On May 26, 2026, Sam Altman stood in front of an audience in Sydney and said something that deserved more attention than it got.

"I'm delighted to be wrong about this," he said. "I thought there would have been more impact on entry-level white-collar jobs being eliminated by now than has actually happened."

The CEO of OpenAI — the company whose technology was supposed to trigger the most significant economic disruption since the Industrial Revolution — just admitted he was wrong about the disruption he spent years predicting.

You'd expect this to be a story about AI failing. It isn't. It's something stranger and more interesting: a story about why the people who built the revolution are admitting it hasn't delivered *yet* — while simultaneously pouring more money into it than ever before, even as the physical world is starting to push back in ways nobody planned for.

And somewhere in the middle of all this, the average knowledge worker is trying to figure out what it means for them.

---

## The Walk-Back Nobody Wanted to Notice

Altman wasn't alone.

The week before his Sydney appearance, Dario Amodei — the CEO of Anthropic, who had previously claimed AI could eliminate 50% of white-collar jobs — quietly reframed his entire position. He now describes AI as an expansion of work rather than a replacement of it:

> "If you automate 90% of the job, then everyone does the 10% of the job… And the 10% kind of expands to be 100% of what people do and kind of 10-times their productivity."

That's the Jevons Paradox dressed up in tech-CEO language. It's a significant intellectual reversal from someone who was predicting mass displacement six months earlier.

Andy Jassy made an even more striking U-turn. In June 2025, he sent an internal memo at Amazon implying the company would need fewer employees because of "efficiency gains" from AI. Four months later, after announcing 14,000 corporate layoffs, he told analysts on Amazon's earnings call: "The announcement that we made a few days ago was not really financially driven, and it's not even really AI-driven — not right now, at least. Really, it's culture."

Amazon then announced 16,000 more layoffs in January 2026.

At Davos in January, Satya Nadella of Microsoft — who had spent two years talking about AI transforming productivity — acknowledged that only 10-12% of companies had seen any revenue or cost benefits from AI, while 56% reported getting nothing from it. Then Jensen Huang, the CEO whose company has profited more than any other from the AI arms race, stood at a Cisco summit in February and said something remarkable: when asked about ROI from AI strategy, he replied, "I get questions like… ROI. I wouldn't go there."

The CEO of the company selling the hardware that AI runs on is telling customers to stop asking whether it's working.

Here's what the data that forced these walk-backs actually shows: a survey of nearly 6,000 executives across four countries, published as an NBER working paper in early 2026, found that 89% of executives reported AI had no measurable impact on productivity. 90% said it had no impact on employment. Goldman Sachs, in a March 2026 report, stated plainly: "We still do not find a meaningful relationship between productivity and AI adoption at the economy-wide level."

The productivity revolution that was going to transform every knowledge worker's job? Still waiting, statistically speaking.

## So Why Is Everyone Spending More?

Here's where the story gets interesting.

In the same quarter these walk-backs were happening, the world's five largest technology companies committed to spending approximately $602 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 alone — a 36% increase from the prior year. Microsoft is spending $80 billion. Meta is spending $80-100 billion. Alphabet is spending $175-185 billion. A joint venture between SoftBank, OpenAI, and Oracle called Project Stargate has committed $500 billion over four years.

The gap between what AI produces today and what this spending requires it to produce is not small. Bain & Company estimates that to justify current investment levels, AI would need to generate $2 trillion in annual revenue by 2030. Actual AI product revenue in 2024 was $45 billion. That's a 44-times gap.

Goldman Sachs analyst James Covello, who has been one of the most pointed skeptics of AI spending, named the psychology driving it: "FOMO has proven a stronger incentive than poor stock performance as hyperscalers have prioritized being involved in the AI arms race over their current shareholders."

Nobody wants to be the company that didn't build the railroad.

But here's the thing — that logic isn't irrational. It's historically defensible.

Between 1998 and 2001, telecom companies laid over 80 million miles of fiber optic cable across the United States, spending more than $100 billion on infrastructure that represented roughly 1% of US GDP. By 2004, 85% of those fiber lines were dark — unused, unlit, derided as the most spectacular waste of capital in American business history.

Then YouTube launched in 2005. Netflix started streaming in 2007. AWS opened in 2006. The bandwidth those "dark fibers" could carry became the foundation for the cloud computing industry, the streaming economy, and much of the modern internet. The investors who went bankrupt in 2001 were right that the infrastructure was necessary — they just died before the applications emerged.

The economist Carlota Perez has a framework for this. Every major technological revolution — steam power, electrification, the internet — follows the same pattern: a speculative frenzy where capital builds infrastructure far ahead of productive use, followed by a financial correction, followed by a deployment phase where productive capital harvests the infrastructure the frenzy built. The people spending $700 billion on AI data centers are placing a bet that their companies won't die before the harvest.

Mark Zuckerberg admitted as much in September 2025: "If we end up misspending a couple hundred billion dollars, that's going to be very unfortunate obviously. But I think the risk of being behind is more concerning to me."

The asymmetry makes the bet rational even when the near-term returns aren't there. If you're right about the long-term, you own the world's most important infrastructure. If you're wrong, you wasted money but didn't disappear. If you don't build and you're wrong about AI not mattering, you're finished.

## Then the Middle East Happened

Here's the part of the story that nobody was modeling.

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched a military operation against Iran. By March 4, Iran had formally closed the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which roughly 25% of the world's seaborne oil and 20% of global LNG normally flows. The IEA called it "the greatest global energy security challenge in history."

The obvious impact: oil prices spiked to $126 per barrel. Dubai crude hit $166 per barrel on March 19. Energy costs rose. Data center operating costs rose with them.

But there was a less obvious impact that didn't make the front pages. It happened because of helium.

Qatar's Ras Laffan Industrial City — the facility that went offline when QatarEnergy declared force majeure in early March — supplies approximately 33% of the world's helium. Helium is not a curiosity. In semiconductor manufacturing, helium cools lithography equipment to cryogenic temperatures and maintains the nanometer-precision clean environments required to etch the patterns on a chip. There is currently no viable substitute in the process. If you don't have helium, you can't make chips.

South Korea imports 65% of its helium from Qatar. Samsung and SK Hynix are the world's largest memory chip manufacturers. Taiwan produces zero helium domestically and imports from the US and Qatar. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Corporation — which makes essentially every leading-edge AI chip in the world, including all Nvidia GPUs, AMD AI accelerators, Google TPUs, and AWS Trainium chips — is on a Taiwan power grid that imports 37% of its LNG from the Gulf. In March, Taiwan had approximately 11 days of LNG reserves.

The knock-on effects were immediate and measurable. Helium prices doubled. Nvidia H200 GPU spot prices rose from $2.27 per hour in January to $3.82 per hour — a 68% increase. DRAM costs, projected at $3.76 per gigabyte in 2025, are now projected at $9.71 per gigabyte for 2026 — a 158% increase.

Think about what that means for the companies spending $700 billion to build AI infrastructure. They wrote those capex projections with 2024 energy prices, 2024 helium availability, and 2024 construction costs. All three are now significantly different. The $600 billion AI buildout is happening — at substantially higher cost than anyone planned for, because of a war in a region that nobody's infrastructure model treated as a variable.

Phil Kornbluth, a helium industry consultant, offered an assessment with no diplomatic veneer: "Your best case scenario would be you're back producing some helium in six weeks or something like that. As it looks right now, that's highly unlikely."

The digital economy runs on physical infrastructure. That physical infrastructure has a supply chain. That supply chain runs through some of the most geopolitically complex geography on earth. Nobody put helium in their $700 billion model, but helium doesn't care.

## What This Means for the Person in the Middle

By now you might be wondering what any of this has to do with you — if you're not a hyperscaler CEO or a helium futures trader.

Here's the part that's actually relevant.

The disruption to the knowledge worker labor market is real, but it's happening in a way that's almost invisible. It's not mass layoffs. It's a hiring freeze that doesn't generate headlines.

Stanford's Digital Economy Lab analyzed ADP payroll data covering millions of workers and found something striking: workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations have seen a 13–16% relative employment decline since ChatGPT's release in late 2022. Software developers in that age group saw approximately a 20% employment drop. But workers aged 30 and older in the same occupations saw 6–12% *growth* in employment over the same period.

Same jobs. Same technology. Opposite outcomes.

Entry-level tech job postings dropped 67% between 2023 and 2024. Entry-level finance positions fell by 24 percentage points. The traditional entry ramp to a knowledge work career — start junior, learn from seniors, earn your way up — is being quietly removed. Not through dramatic layoffs that make news, but through companies that simply stopped hiring the bottom rungs of the ladder.

The research that explains why comes from a peer-reviewed study by Erik Brynjolfsson and colleagues, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in May 2025. They studied 5,172 customer support agents with access to AI assistance. The average productivity gain was 14%. But for novice, low-skilled workers: 34% improvement. For experienced, high-skilled workers: minimal gain, sometimes a slight quality decline.

AI is an expertise equalizer. It makes the novice punch like an expert.

The problem is what companies do with that information. If an AI can take someone with six months of experience and give them the output quality of someone with three years of experience, you don't need as many junior hires. You hire fewer people, assign them more scope, and use AI to cover the coverage gaps. The IESE Business School confirmed this pattern across 138 million US workers: AI is substituting for junior workers while augmenting senior ones. It's been called "seniority-biased technological change." Junior wages have fallen 4.5% in AI-exposed companies since 2023. Senior wages held.

The further irony: Goldman Sachs data shows office and administrative tasks are 46% automatable by current AI. Construction work is 6% automatable. Electrical and installation work: 4%. The knowledge workers who spent a generation assuming their cognitive labor was automation-resistant are now more exposed than the electrician.

This is not a reason for despair. It's a reason to recalibrate.

## The Counterintuitive Playbook

The conventional wisdom about surviving the AI era goes something like: learn to prompt, get AI-certified, become fluent with the tools. That advice is not wrong, exactly, but it's missing the point.

The 56% wage premium documented by PwC's 2026 analysis of nearly one billion job postings goes not to AI-fluent generalists, but to workers who combine deep domain expertise with AI fluency. Pure AI skills without domain context are commoditizing rapidly. The tools keep changing; the models keep improving; anyone can learn to use ChatGPT. What AI cannot currently generate is genuine accumulated judgment in a field — the kind that comes from years of solving real problems in a specific domain.

The practical implication: the highest-value move is not "learn AI." It's "become genuinely expert in something, then add AI on top."

More specifically, Brynjolfsson's finding that AI helps novices most cuts both ways. Yes, as an individual user, AI can accelerate your learning. Use that. The traditional career ladder used to be the mechanism for accumulating expertise — you worked on smaller problems until you earned access to bigger ones. AI can compress that timeline if you use it deliberately: learn what your senior colleagues produce, understand the judgment behind decisions, get exposure to harder problems than the ladder would normally allow. Get to genuine expertise faster than the traditional path would have let you.

There's also a structural protection that most AI-era career advice doesn't mention. Research on automation risk consistently finds four types of work that AI handles poorly, each with a strong negative correlation to automation probability:

Physical unpredictability — work that exists in the physical world and varies in ways that haven't already been seen. An HVAC technician diagnosing a system nobody has documented. A surgeon encountering anatomy that doesn't match the textbook.

Genuine human relationships — not customer service scripts, but the kind of trust-based relationship where the relationship itself is what the client is paying for. Therapists, account managers with deep client history, advisors who know things about you that you haven't told a search engine.

Creative judgment beyond pattern matching — not just generating content, but making judgment calls in genuinely novel situations where there's no prior data distribution to learn from.

Ethical and legal accountability — licensed liability that cannot be delegated to a machine. The doctor who signs the chart. The lawyer who advises the strategy. The engineer who stamps the plans.

If your current work touches at least two of these dimensions, you have structural protection. If it touches none, the disruption that's already removing entry-level positions will eventually reach you.

The final piece of advice is the hardest to accept: stop treating this as a temporary disruption that will settle. The electrician is safer than the paralegal right now. That might reverse in ten years, when AI systems get better at physical world tasks. The goal isn't to find the permanently safe category — there isn't one. The goal is to stay in motion: accumulate expertise faster than the automation boundary can advance, keep the judgment layer in your work irreducibly human, and build relationships that can't be prompted into existence.

## The Longer Arc

Robert Solow said in 1987 that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." He wasn't wrong — computers were real, the personal computer had transformed how people worked, but the economy-wide productivity boost didn't show up until the mid-1990s. Fifteen years later.

Erik Brynjolfsson, who spent a career studying that productivity gap, is the same economist now arguing that 2025's US productivity jump of 2.7% — nearly double the decade's average — is the first macro-level signal that the AI harvest has begun. "We are transitioning from an era of AI experimentation to one of structural utility," he wrote in February 2026.

Maybe. Or maybe it's noise. Daron Acemoglu — who won a Nobel Prize in economics — estimates AI will automate only about 5% of tasks within ten years, generating roughly 0.5% cumulative GDP growth. "I don't think we should belittle 0.5% in 10 years," he said, in what may be the most carefully calibrated sentence in modern economics. "That's better than zero. But it's just disappointing relative to the promises."

The serious economists disagree. The CEOs are walking back their claims while doubling their capex. The supply chain is running through a war zone. And somewhere in all of this, the 24-year-old trying to get their first job in tech is experiencing a 20% employment decline in their cohort while nobody is talking about it as a crisis.

The AI revolution is real. The timeline is uncertain. The physical constraints are underestimated. And the disruption to individual workers is already happening — just not in the way anyone was watching for.

Sam Altman said he was "delighted to be wrong" about AI eliminating entry-level jobs. He probably is. But the data suggests he was wrong about the mechanism, not the direction. The jobs aren't being eliminated through dramatic AI replacements. They're being eliminated through the quieter, less newsworthy act of companies that simply stopped creating them.

That distinction matters. Because it means the response isn't "don't worry, AI isn't taking your job." The response is: the ladder is being removed, the harvest is still years away, and the best thing you can do right now is sprint to the rung above the one that's disappearing.

# Newsletter Draft: The People Selling the AI Revolution Just Admitted It Isn't Working Yet
**Date**: 2026-06-09
**Source**: article-ai-ceo-backtrack-hormuz-employee-survival-2026-06-09.md

---

SUBJECT LINE:
They admitted AI isn't working. Then spent $700B.

PREVIEW TEXT:
The walk-backs got quiet. The spending got louder.

EMAIL BODY:
---

For two years, the story was simple: AI was coming for white-collar work, fast, and you should be terrified.

I half-believed it. You probably did too.

So here's what genuinely threw me this spring. The exact people who sold that story started quietly taking it back.

Sam Altman, May 26: "I'm delighted to be wrong about this. I thought there would have been more impact on entry-level white-collar jobs being eliminated by now than has actually happened."

Dario Amodei -- who once predicted AI would erase half of white-collar jobs -- now talks about automation *expanding* work. Andy Jassy said Amazon's layoffs were "not really AI-driven... really, it's culture." Even Jensen Huang, asked about ROI, said plainly: "I wouldn't go there."

When the loudest believers start hedging, it's worth asking why.

**Here's what the data actually shows.**

It's not subtle. An NBER survey of roughly 6,000 executives found 89% saw no measurable productivity impact from AI. Goldman Sachs, in March, found "no meaningful relationship between productivity and AI adoption at the economy-wide level." Gartner found 80% of companies cut their workforce after AI pilots -- with zero correlation to higher returns.

Satya Nadella put a number on it at Davos: only 10-12% of companies see any benefit. 56% get nothing.

So the revolution is stalling, the executives are admitting it, and the obvious next move would be to stop spending.

They're doing the opposite. Over $700 billion is going into AI infrastructure this year.

**Which brings me to the part most people miss.**

The spending isn't denial. It's a specific, deliberate bet -- and it has a precedent.

Remember the late-90s fiber boom? Companies laid enormous amounts of fiber-optic cable. By 2004, 85% of it sat unused -- "dark fiber," a monument to overinvestment. Except that same dark fiber later powered YouTube, Netflix, and AWS. The frenzy looked insane in real time and prescient in hindsight.

The economist Carlota Perez named this pattern: speculative capital overbuilds the infrastructure first; productive capital harvests it years later. The waste is the cost of being early.

The math is wild. By 2030, AI needs to generate around $2 trillion in revenue to justify today's spend. Current AI revenue: about $45 billion. That's a 44x gap.

So why keep going? Because the bet is asymmetric. If you don't build and AI turns out to matter -- you're finished. If you build and it doesn't -- you wasted money but you survive. As Goldman's Covello put it, "FOMO has proven a stronger incentive than poor stock performance." It's not optimism. It's insurance against extinction.

**Now layer in the thing almost no one is pricing in.**

When the Strait of Hormuz closed in March, 25% of the world's seaborne oil and 20% of its LNG stopped moving. Hidden inside that: Qatar's Ras Laffan supplies a third of the world's helium -- which has *no substitute* in chip manufacturing. Taiwan, which makes every leading-edge AI chip on earth, imports 97% of its energy and held roughly 11 days of reserves.

Helium prices doubled. H200 GPU spot prices jumped 68%. DRAM is projected up 158% this year. The AI buildout assumes a calm supply chain. That assumption just cracked.

**So what does any of this mean for you on a Tuesday?**

The honest takeaway isn't "AI will take your job" or "AI is a bubble, relax." It's narrower and more useful than both.

The job market is already splitting -- quietly. Stanford and ADP data shows tech workers aged 22-25 down 20% in employment, while workers 30+ in the same roles grew 12%. Entry-level tech postings fell 67%. Nobody's getting laid off in waves. The *ladder* is being removed. Hiring freezes, not pink slips.

So three concrete moves:

First, stop chasing "AI fluency" as a standalone skill. PwC found the real premium -- 56% on wages -- goes to people who pair domain expertise *with* AI fluency. AI fluency alone is becoming table stakes.

Second, weight toward what AI can't touch yet: physical unpredictability, genuine human relationships, judgment beyond pattern-matching, and accountability someone has to legally own. Goldman pegs office/admin work as 46% automatable. Electricians? 6%. The electrician really is safer than the paralegal right now.

Third -- the counterintuitive one. Brynjolfsson's research found AI helps novices most (+34%) by spreading the best practices of expert workers. So use it as a compression tool. The thing eroding the bottom of the ladder is also the fastest way to climb it. Let it teach you to think like a senior person three years early.

That's the move I keep coming back to. The people removing the ladder accidentally handed everyone a faster way up it. Most won't use it that way.

I keep wondering whether that's optimism or denial on my part. Genuinely unsure. If you've found a third reading, I'd love to hear it.

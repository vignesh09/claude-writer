## EMAIL BODY

---

You've probably seen the story: a CEO spent three weeks living in the office, bought his team mattresses, while his industry laid off tens of thousands of people. Read as one story, it's hypocrisy.

Except it isn't one story. It's two different founders, and once you separate them, the real pattern is more interesting than "CEO bad."

---

### The two mattresses

Nico Laqua, CEO of Corgi, sleeps three hours a night on his office floor, seven days a week. His employees are expected to do the same — "a rest day every now and then" is the closest thing to time off. Corgi just raised a Series B1 at a $2.6B valuation.

Matan Grinberg, CEO of Factory, did the opposite. He bought all 30 of his employees (now 120+) a $3,000 smart mattress cover — not to demand more hours, but to optimize the sleep they already get. He called it "the decadence of startups."

Neither of them laid anyone off. The layoffs are happening somewhere else, for a different reason.

---

### The layoffs aren't really about capability

~150,000 tech jobs cut in 2026 already — more than all of 2025. AI has been the #1 stated reason for four straight months. Meta grew revenue 33% year over year and still cut 8,000 jobs while raising AI spending to $115-145B.

Even Sam Altman undercut the story everyone assumes: "Almost every company that does layoffs is blaming AI, whether or not it really is about AI."

Cloudflare's CEO was more direct about who actually got cut: "the vast majority... were measurers" — middle management, finance, legal, audit.

---

### The actual pattern

Here's what clicked for me: the mattress-on-the-floor and the mattress-for-everyone aren't opposite instincts. They're the *same* instinct — overinvest in whatever's scarce and hard to replace — pointed at two different asset classes.

The core (frontier researchers, irreplaceable generalists) gets treated like scarce capital equipment: you overspend to keep it running because replacing it costs more. A $90K mattress budget is nothing next to a $2M researcher search.

The periphery (legible, describable-in-a-job-posting roles) gets treated like elastic cloud compute: scaled down the moment a cheaper substitute exists, real or not, because the switching cost is close to zero.

This isn't hypocrisy. Hypocrisy is something a company gets embarrassed out of. This is a strategy, said out loud, in earnings calls — because saying it costs nothing anymore.

---

**So the question I keep coming back to isn't about any CEO. It's about the work itself: is what you do closer to "amplifies expert judgment" (PwC's data says that group gets 2x the job growth, 42% faster raises) or closer to "substitutable output"?**

Hit reply and tell me — which side of that line does your role sit on right now, honestly?

I read every response.

# Why AI Subscriptions May Not Be Economically Sustainable

## Subject line options

1. The AI pricing story nobody wants to tell
2. Why “cheap” AI probably isn’t cheap
3. The subsidy behind your AI subscription

## Preview text

The current pricing looks generous because someone else is paying for the burn. That probably won’t last.

---

I think the AI subscription story is being misread.

The market is treating today’s pricing like proof that AI is affordable. But what if the opposite is true? What if current AI subscriptions are not an economically stable price — just a temporarily subsidized one?

That matters, because there’s a big difference between “this tool is cheap” and “this tool is being sold cheaply.”

Right now, a lot of AI pricing looks less like a normal business model and more like a land grab.

The companies behind these products are absorbing enormous costs:

- model training
- inference at scale
- GPU infrastructure
- networking and storage
- safety and moderation
- support for enterprise customers
- constant model iteration

Those costs do not disappear because the sticker price is $20, $30, or even $200 a month.

They’re just being hidden somewhere else.

And that “somewhere else” is the real story.

In the consumer phase, the subsidy is often paid for by investors. In the platform phase, it’s paid for by hyperscalers who want to own the stack. In the enterprise phase, it may be paid for by bundling, credits, or strategic loss-leader pricing designed to lock in behavior before the bill arrives.

That’s why cheap AI can feel almost too good to be true.

Because in many cases, it is.

The current pricing assumes a world where usage stays moderate, answer quality stays high, latency stays low, and customers don’t fully exploit the system. But as usage rises, economics change fast. Every extra prompt costs something. Every long context window costs something. Every multimodal request costs something. Every agentic workflow that loops, retries, and calls tools costs more than the neat subscription fee suggests.

In other words: AI is not software in the old sense. It is software with a variable marginal cost attached to every interaction.

That breaks the old SaaS logic.

Traditional software gets cheaper as it scales because the product is copied, not consumed. AI gets more expensive as it’s used. The more valuable it becomes in practice, the more it can cost to serve.

That creates a strange tension.

On paper, AI looks like it should replace labor because the subscription is cheaper than a salary. In reality, that comparison is usually misleading.

A salary is not just cost. It’s capacity, judgment, accountability, context, and institutional memory.

An AI subscription is not just a cheaper worker. It is a probabilistic service with hidden overhead:

- human review
- prompt maintenance
- workflow design
- error correction
- compliance risk
- integration work
- duplicated effort when outputs are wrong

If you include those costs honestly, the “AI is cheaper than an employee” argument gets much weaker.

And that’s before you ask the harder question: what happens when the subsidy ends?

Usually one of three things happens:

1. Prices go up.
2. Usage limits go down.
3. Quality gets tiered.

That’s the normal ending to heavily subsidized products. The market experiments with generosity, locks in behavior, then extracts margin later.

We’ve seen this pattern before with cloud services, ride-hailing, delivery apps, and streaming bundles. The front-end price looked simple. The real economics were anything but.

AI may follow the same path.

The first phase teaches users to depend on it. The second phase monetizes that dependence.

That doesn’t mean AI is bad business. It means the current price is probably not the final price.

So the contrarian view is not that AI won’t be valuable. It’s that many current subscriptions are priced like a growth strategy, not like a sustainable equilibrium.

If you’re using AI today, enjoy the subsidy. It is real.

But don’t confuse a subsidized price with a stable one. And don’t confuse a subscription with the full cost of production.

The interesting question isn’t whether AI is useful.

It’s whether the economics of “cheap AI” can survive once the market stops pretending compute is free.

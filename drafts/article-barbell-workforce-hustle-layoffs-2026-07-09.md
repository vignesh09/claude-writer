You've probably heard some version of this story: a startup CEO spent three weeks basically living in the office, and — in the same breath — bought his employees mattresses, while his industry quietly laid off tens of thousands of people. Everyone's read it as hypocrisy: the "we're a family" CEO who works you to the bone with one hand and hands out pillows with the other, right before the layoff memo lands.

Here's the thing. That's not one story. It's three, and it's more interesting once you separate them.

The mattress-on-the-office-floor story belongs to Nico Laqua, CEO of a startup called Corgi. He works seven days a week and sleeps roughly three hours a night on the floor of his own office. His employees are also expected to work seven days a week — "a rest day every now and then" is the closest thing to a break. Corgi hit unicorn status, then raised a Series B1 at a $2.6 billion valuation in the same month. A well-known VC podcast host called it "the most intense workplace culture in America."

The mattress-for-employees story belongs to someone else entirely: Matan Grinberg, a physicist-turned-founder who runs an AI startup called Factory. He bought all 30 of his employees (the company has since grown past 120) a $3,000 smart mattress cover — the kind that actively cools or heats itself to your ideal sleep temperature. He called it, only half-joking, "the decadence of startups." His stated reasoning wasn't "work harder." It was sleep optimization — squeezing more out of the brains he already had, not demanding more hours from them.

Two founders. Two companies. Two opposite philosophies — one imposes sacrifice downward, the other invests in recovery. Neither of them laid anyone off. The layoffs are happening somewhere else entirely, at a much bigger scale, for reasons that have almost nothing to do with either of these guys.

**And that's the part worth sitting with.**

## The layoffs are real, and they're not really about capability

Roughly 150,000 tech jobs have been cut in 2026 already — more than all of 2025, and we're not through July. AI has been the number one stated reason for four straight months, a streak with no precedent in the data. Meta posted $56.3 billion in Q1 revenue, up 33% year over year, and still cut 8,000 jobs in May — while raising its AI infrastructure spending guidance to $115–145 billion for the year. Internal communications reportedly described the cuts as a way to help offset the cost of that AI buildout.

Cloudflare's CEO, Matthew Prince, was unusually blunt about who got cut in his company's 1,100-person layoff: "the vast majority of those we laid off... were measurers" — middle management, finance, legal, internal audit, revenue recognition. Microsoft's 9,000-person round explicitly targeted middle management and customer-success teams "to accelerate investment in AI engineering." Google has cut more than a third of the managers overseeing small teams.

Here's the detail that undoes the tidy "AI is coming for jobs" narrative, though. It comes from the person with the most incentive to say AI is unstoppable: OpenAI's Sam Altman admitted, twice this year, that "almost every company that does layoffs is blaming AI, whether or not it really is about AI."

Read that again. The industry's most prominent AI evangelist is telling you the capability story is often just cover for something else.

## What the something else actually is

Meanwhile, at the top of the same industry, pay is doing something wild. PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer — built on more than a billion job postings across 27 countries — found the labor market "is no longer adjusting to AI. It is dividing along it." Roles where AI *amplifies* expert judgment (their example: radiologists, recruiters) are seeing twice the job growth and 42% faster salary growth than roles where AI simply *substitutes* for the person doing them.

The spread at the extreme end is almost cartoonish. The government's median wage baseline sits around $133,000. OpenAI's median total compensation is somewhere around $795,000 to $870,000. Frontier-lab packages for senior researchers routinely run $500,000 to $2 million once you count equity. That's not a raise. That's a different economy.

Put the two halves next to each other and the picture stops looking like hypocrisy and starts looking like a plan. Companies aren't confused about whether to treat people as precious or disposable. They've split their workforce into two entirely different asset classes and they're running two entirely different playbooks — on purpose, out loud, in earnings calls.

The core — the frontier researcher, the systems architect, the person whose judgment can't be cleanly specified into a prompt — gets treated like scarce capital equipment. You overspend to keep it running, because replacing it is slower and costlier than maintaining it. A $3,000 mattress cover, multiplied across a whole team, is a rounding error next to the cost and delay of recruiting a single replacement researcher at a million-dollar package with a six-month search. It isn't generosity. It's depreciation math.

The periphery — the "measurer," the generalist manager, the role whose value is legible enough to describe in a job posting an AI tool can now approximate — gets treated like elastic cloud compute. You scale it down the moment a cheaper substitute exists, or even looks plausible enough to justify to the board, because the switching cost is close to zero.

Sleeping on the office floor and buying everyone a smart mattress *look* like opposite instincts. They're actually the same instinct pointed at the same kind of asset: over-invest in whatever's genuinely scarce, whatever compounds, whatever's expensive to replace. The layoffs are the mirror image of that exact logic, aimed at everything that isn't.

## Why the "hypocrisy" framing lets everyone off the hook

Calling this hypocrisy feels satisfying, but it's actually a mercy. Hypocrisy implies an inconsistency — something a company could get embarrassed out of, or that outrage could shame into changing. A coherent strategy doesn't work that way. You don't shame a spreadsheet.

And this genuinely is a stated strategy, not a subtext you have to squint to see. Cloudflare's CEO told you, on the record, who counted as a "measurer." Microsoft told you the middle-management cuts were explicitly to fund AI engineering. Meta's own internal messaging reportedly framed the layoffs as offsetting AI capex — while the company was growing revenue 33% year over year. This isn't a mask slipping. It's the plan, said out loud, because saying it out loud doesn't cost these companies anything anymore.

The Gini coefficient just hit a 60-year high. Labor's share of GDP is at its lowest level in 78 years. Those aren't side effects of a temporary AI hiccup working itself out. They're the visible signature of exactly the sorting mechanism described above, running at scale, for the first time with this much data behind it.

## What actually moves, if you're not a frontier researcher

None of this is really an argument about the ethics of any individual CEO — Laqua's extreme culture and Grinberg's expensive one are both, honestly, sideshows next to the real story. The real question the barbell workforce raises for anyone reading this at their own job is narrower and more useful: which side of the split is your actual work on?

The PwC data draws the line more precisely than "AI will take your job" ever does. It's not about whether AI touches your role. Almost every role gets touched now. It's about whether AI *amplifies* your judgment or *substitutes* for your output — whether what you do is illegible enough, compounding enough, expensive enough to replace that a company would rather build you a better sleep schedule than write you off in a quarter's cost-cutting round.

That's not a comfortable place to end an article. It's not supposed to be. But it's a more accurate map of what's actually happening than "your boss is a hypocrite" — and a more actionable one, too.

What would it take to move your own work from the legible column to the illegible one — from something a job posting can fully describe to something a company can't easily replace? I don't think there's a clean formula. But I think that's the actual question hiding underneath the mattress story, and it's worth asking honestly.

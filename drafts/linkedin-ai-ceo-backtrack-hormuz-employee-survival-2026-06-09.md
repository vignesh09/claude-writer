Everyone keeps asking whether AI will take their job.

I think that's the wrong question.

Last month, Sam Altman stood on a stage in Sydney and said: "I'm delighted to be wrong about this. I thought there would have been more impact on entry-level white-collar jobs being eliminated by now."

The CEO of OpenAI admitting the disruption he spent years predicting hasn't really arrived.

He wasn't alone. Amodei walked back his 50% job-loss prediction. Jassy said Amazon's layoffs were "culture, not AI." Even Jensen Huang, asked about returns on AI spending, said: "I wouldn't go there."

So the threat is overblown? That's what I assumed too. Then I looked at who's actually getting hit.

Here's the part that changed my thinking.

Tech workers aged 22-25 saw their employment fall 20%. Workers 30 and older in the same field? Employment grew 12%. Entry-level job postings are down 67%.

It's not that AI is replacing people. It's replacing the bottom rung of the ladder.

Stanford research (Brynjolfsson, QJE) found the same pattern across industries: junior wages dropped 4.5% while senior wages held flat. The machine isn't coming for experience. It's coming for the path you used to take to get experience.

That reframes the whole problem.

The danger was never "will AI replace me." It's "will AI quietly remove the steps I was counting on to become harder to replace."

Which means the move isn't to learn more AI tools. It's to use them to sprint toward seniority faster than the ladder disappears.

I'm still working out what that looks like in practice.

If you're early in your career right now, what are you doing to close the experience gap faster than it's being erased?

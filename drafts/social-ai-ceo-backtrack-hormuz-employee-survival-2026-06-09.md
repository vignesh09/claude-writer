## Post 1 — Single Observation

Same week Sam Altman admitted he was "pretty wrong" about AI eliminating jobs, GPU prices jumped 68%.

The reason wasn't demand. It was a war in the Middle East cutting off helium -- Qatar supplies a third of the world's chip-making helium.

The digital economy just ran into the physical one.

## Post 2 — Counterintuitive Reframe (thread opener, 3 tweets)

1/ Everyone's bracing for AI to take their job in a wave of layoffs.

That's not how it's happening.

The jobs aren't being eliminated. They're quietly not being created. The bottom rung of the ladder is just... gone.

2/ The data is brutal in its specificity:

Tech workers aged 22-25: employment down 20%.
Same roles, workers 30+: employment UP 12%.

Same jobs. Same AI. Opposite outcomes.

Entry-level tech postings are down 67%.

3/ Here's the cruel irony: AI helps novices MOST -- a 34% productivity boost for low-skill workers.

So companies stopped hiring novices.

They're not removing the people on the ladder. They're removing the ladder.

# Podcast Q&A Script

Style note: curious, not leading. Each question hands the guest room to say something honest and slightly uncomfortable. Diary-of-a-CEO register -- direct, a little vulnerable, never preachy.

## Exchange 1 — The Admission

**Host:** "Sam Altman stood on a stage and said he was delighted to be wrong about AI eliminating jobs. When you read that -- did you take it as humility, or as a tell?"

**Answer direction:** Resist the easy cynical read. Sit in the tension: it's probably genuine humility AND a tell at the same time. He was wrong about the *mechanism* -- dramatic replacements -- but the direction was right. The jobs are disappearing, just through hiring freezes nobody live-tweets. The interesting move is admitting the prediction failed while spending more than ever. That's not contradiction; that's conviction. Land on: an admission of being wrong about timing is not the same as being wrong about the thing.

## Exchange 2 — The Part Nobody Saw Coming

**Host:** "There's this detail in the story I can't stop thinking about. A war in the Middle East ends up spiking GPU prices -- through helium of all things. Walk me through how that's even possible."

**Answer direction:** This is the gift of the conversation -- let it breathe. Qatar offline, force majeure, a third of the world's helium gone. No substitute exists in chip lithography. H200 prices up 68%, DRAM up 158%. The deeper point: the $700B capex models were written with 2024 assumptions, and nobody put helium in the spreadsheet. The lesson isn't "predict helium next time." It's that the digital supply chain is an analog supply chain wearing a hoodie. The cloud has a coastline.

## Exchange 3 — The Bet That Looks Insane

**Host:** "Eighty-nine percent of executives say AI hasn't moved their productivity at all. And the spending is going UP. Help me understand how that's anything other than a bubble."

**Answer direction:** Be honest that it might be a bubble -- don't dodge. But then complicate it. The dark fiber story: telecoms laid 80 million miles of cable in '98, 85% sat dark by 2004, everyone called it the dumbest capital waste in history. Then YouTube, Netflix, AWS arrived and that "waste" became the internet. The investors were right about the infrastructure -- they just died before the applications showed up. So the real question isn't "is this rational?" It's "can these companies survive long enough to be right?" The asymmetry makes the bet defensible even when the returns aren't there yet.

## Exchange 4 — The Quiet Crisis

**Host:** "You make this point that there's a 24-year-old out there experiencing a 20% employment drop in their cohort, and nobody's calling it a crisis. Why isn't anyone watching this?"

**Answer direction:** Because it doesn't look like a crisis. Crises have headlines -- mass layoffs, a name, a villain. This is the absence of a job posting, which generates no news. The ladder is being removed one rung at a time, silently. Get vulnerable here: the people most exposed are exactly the ones who were told knowledge work was the safe bet. The paralegal is now less protected than the electrician -- 46% of office tasks are automatable versus 4% of installation work. Don't let it tip into despair. The point is recalibration, not doom.

## Exchange 5 — What You'd Actually Tell Someone

**Host:** "Okay -- so if a young person in tech came to you genuinely worried, and you only had thirty seconds, what's the real advice? Not the LinkedIn version."

**Answer direction:** Kill the conventional advice first: "learn to prompt" is nearly worthless on its own -- pure AI skill is commoditizing by the month. The 56% wage premium goes to deep domain expertise PLUS AI, not AI alone. So the move is counterintuitive: become genuinely, irreducibly expert at something real, then layer AI on top. Use AI to climb faster than the old ladder allowed -- it compresses the learning curve. And stop hunting for the permanently safe category; there isn't one. The electrician is safe today, maybe not in ten years. The only real strategy is staying in motion -- accumulate judgment faster than the automation line can advance. End on the article's closing image: sprint to the rung above the one that's disappearing.

I find it hard to write about economic opportunity when people are dying.

Iran-UAE. Ukraine. Gaza. Lebanon. Real people losing everything across three continents right now.

And yet -- every time I study a past crisis, I find the same pattern. Opportunity doesn't disappear during conflict. It *relocates*. And pretending otherwise doesn't help anyone.

So I'm going to make a case today that might feel wrong at first. Bear with me.

---

**The Airbnb bet nobody talks about correctly**

In April 2020, Silver Lake Partners invested $1 billion in Airbnb.

At the time, people called it reckless. Travel was literally illegal in most countries. Airbnb had cut 25% of its workforce. Its valuation had collapsed from $31 billion to $18 billion. The hospitality industry looked structurally destroyed.

Silver Lake's co-CEO said Airbnb was "particularly well suited to prosper as the world *inevitably* recovers."

That word -- *inevitably* -- is worth sitting with. He wasn't predicting the timing. He was asserting the direction.

Eight months later, Airbnb went public at $68 billion. Silver Lake's paper gains: roughly $460 million, on top of double-digit interest they'd already collected.

The lesson isn't about Silver Lake's genius. It's about what happens when you bet on structural recovery while everyone else is pricing in permanent catastrophe.

> "The question isn't whether the wealth transfer happens during a crisis. It's which side you're on."

---

**Two things that stay true no matter what**

I've spent time studying crises not for trading tips, but to find the invariants. What stays true regardless?

**Reconstruction always follows destruction -- and the window opens before the peace deal.**

The World Bank estimates Ukraine's reconstruction at $588 billion over the next decade. That's not a crisis number. That's one of the largest construction projects in modern history.

Post-WWII: the Marshall Plan rebuilt Europe and funded the American suburban boom. Post-COVID: remote work infrastructure, telemedicine, and mental health all became $50B+ industries. The pattern is consistent: destruction creates reconstruction demand, and the people who position *during* the conflict profit *from* the rebuilding.

Here's the part most people miss: after every peace deal, prices reset higher. The buying window isn't after the peace. It's before it.

**Human nature doesn't pause during crisis -- it redirects.**

COVID killed more than 7 million people. The global economy contracted by trillions. And yet:

- Birth rates rose 6.2% among work-from-home-eligible women
- Remote work went from 17% to 44% overnight, then stabilized at 43% hybrid -- permanently
- Entrepreneurs started businesses at record rates in 2020 and 2021

People don't stop wanting things during crisis. They want different things, in different ways, from different places. The economic activity doesn't disappear. It redirects.

---

**The translation table that changed my thinking**

This is the frame that unlocked everything for me. Look at what COVID looked like in real-time versus what it actually created:

- "Travel industry destroyed" became Airbnb's $68B IPO
- "Mental health crisis" became a $5B+ app market
- "Supply chain collapse" became a $300B+ tech investment wave
- "Office real estate dying" became a suburban and secondary city boom

Now translate today's crises the same way:

- European energy dependency on Russia becomes a trillion-euro green energy investment
- Defense tech urgency becomes the next civilian AI and autonomous systems wave
- Ukraine destroyed becomes a $588B reconstruction pipeline
- Supply chains rerouting around conflict becomes a Central Asia, Turkey, India logistics boom

The silver linings are never visible in real-time. They require the same lens Silver Lake used: *where is this inevitably going?*

---

**What this means if you're not a billion-dollar fund**

The most common objection is fair: "Silver Lake had $20 billion in reserves. I have a savings account."

True. And it changes the scale, not the principle.

Three things anyone can do:

**Keep cash available.** A 10-20% cash buffer isn't underperformance -- it's optionality. Crisis opportunities require liquidity, not leverage.

**Make a list now of assets you'd buy if they dropped 30%.** Not speculatively. Assets you understand fundamentally and would hold for 5-10 years. Crisis is not the time to discover new investments -- it's the time to buy what you've always wanted at the prices you've always wanted.

**Stop selling what you planned to hold.** The most destructive behavior during crisis is panic selling of fundamentally sound assets. Silver Lake didn't sell when everyone else was dumping travel stocks. They bought.

> "The fire is always real. The ash always grows things."

---

**The harder question**

I started by saying I find it uncomfortable to write about opportunity during genuine suffering. I still do.

But I've come to believe the discomfort is important, not paralyzing. Capital that flows toward reconstruction *matters*. Silver Lake's $1 billion in Airbnb wasn't charity -- but it kept the company alive as an employer of hundreds of thousands of hosts. Crisis capital has a moral dimension that pure profit-seeking obscures.

When the world is on fire, the people who move toward the smoke -- with capital, with patience, with conviction about inevitable recovery -- are the ones who shape what gets rebuilt.

The alternative -- waiting on the sidelines for stability -- means someone else decides what gets built.

Post-WWII ash grew the Marshall Plan and 30 years of the broadest prosperity in human history. Post-COVID ash grew remote work and a permanent reorientation of how we relate to work and place. Post-conflict ash will grow something too.

The question history keeps asking is the same one: *Were you positioned to plant in the ash, or were you still mourning the fire?*

Let me say something uncomfortable upfront: I find it hard to write about economic opportunity in a world where people are dying.

The Iran-UAE conflict. Ukraine. Gaza. Lebanon. Ongoing flashpoints across three continents. Real people losing everything.

And yet — every time I look at history, I find the same pattern. Crisis does not destroy opportunity. It *relocates* it. And the people who understand where it moves — not out of cold calculation, but out of genuine pattern recognition — are the ones who end up funding the rebuilding.

So I'm going to make a case that might feel wrong at first: **the world being on fire is not evidence that things are getting worse. It might be evidence that something is being forged.**

This isn't optimism. It's history.

---

## The Airbnb Story Everyone Gets Wrong

In April 2020, Silver Lake Partners and Sixth Street made one of the most counterintuitive bets in recent financial history.

Airbnb's business had essentially ceased to exist. Travel was illegal in most countries. Airbnb had cut 25% of its workforce, and its valuation had collapsed from $31 billion in 2017 to roughly $18 billion. The company was burning cash. Nobody wanted to touch it.

Silver Lake invested $1 billion anyway.

People at the time called it reckless. The travel industry wasn't just struggling — it looked structurally destroyed. Who would ever feel safe sleeping in a stranger's house again? The hospitality industry was over.

Silver Lake Co-CEO Egon Durban didn't see it that way. He said Airbnb's model was "particularly well suited to prosper as the world inevitably recovers."

*Inevitably.*

That word is worth sitting with. He wasn't predicting the timing. He was asserting the direction.

By December 2020 — eight months later — Airbnb went public at $68 billion. Silver Lake and Sixth Street each held paper gains of approximately $460 million on their investment, *on top of* double-digit interest they'd already earned on the debt portion of the deal. They negotiated terms — warrants, high interest rates, equity kickers — that are only available when everyone else is paralyzed by fear.

The lesson isn't about Silver Lake's genius. It's about what happens when you bet on structural recovery while everyone else is pricing in permanent catastrophe.

## Why Crisis Is Always a Wealth-Transfer Event

Here's the uncomfortable truth most financial commentary avoids:

**Every major crisis in modern history has been a wealth-transfer event.**

Not to the lucky. To the *prepared and contrarian*.

This isn't a moral statement. It's a mechanical one. When fear causes assets to be priced at distress levels, capital flows from those who panic to those who don't. The question isn't whether the transfer happens. It's which side you're on.

The people who bought US equities during the 2008 financial crisis (when Warren Buffett publicly invested in Goldman Sachs) made 400% over the next decade. The people who moved to cash "until things settled down" missed the entire recovery.

The people who bought New York real estate during COVID's "cities are dead" panic of 2020 made 40%+ within 3 years.

The people who bought Airbnb stock at IPO — trusting that travel would return — turned $68 into over $200 within 18 months.

None of this required special intelligence. It required one thing: **the ability to distinguish between temporary crisis and permanent change.**

## Five Ground Truths That War (and Pandemic, and Recession) Cannot Change

I've spent time studying crises — not to find trading tips, but because I wanted to understand what the invariants are. What stays true no matter what?

Here are five things I'm now confident about:

### 1. Capital Always Moves Toward Safety. But "Safety" Shifts.

During WWII, people fled to Swiss francs. During the Cold War, US Treasuries. During 2008, gold. During COVID, tech stocks and suburban real estate.

The flight-to-safety behavior is constant. The *destination* of that flight changes with every crisis. And here's the thing: wherever global capital is running *to* right now is both the opportunity and the trap.

Dubai's property market hit $250 billion in transactions in 2025 — a record. Iranian drone strikes in early 2026 dropped the Dubai Property Index by 20% and introduced 2-7% discounts at closing. Capital spooked.

But where does that capital go? Not back to London, with its political instability. Not to New York, with its legal uncertainty. It circulates among a very small number of "safe" addresses: Singapore. Switzerland. Certain corners of the UAE. Parts of the US.

The money doesn't disappear. It moves. And it moves predictably.

### 2. Reconstruction Always Follows Destruction — And the Opportunity Is Before the Peace Deal

The World Bank estimates Ukraine's reconstruction will cost $588 billion over the next decade. That's not a crisis number. That's a construction project number — one of the largest in modern history.

Post-WWII: The Marshall Plan rebuilt Europe, triggered the Japanese economic miracle, and funded the American suburban boom.
Post-Gulf War: Kuwait reconstruction rebuilt an entire economy.
Post-COVID: Remote work infrastructure, telemedicine, and mental health sectors all became $50B+ industries.

The pattern is consistent: destruction creates reconstruction demand, and the people who position *during* the conflict profit *from* the reconstruction.

Here's what the pattern also shows: after every peace deal, prices reset higher. The buying window isn't after the peace. It's before it.

### 3. War Is the World's Most Expensive R&D Program — Paid by Governments, Collected by Civilians

This one surprises people.

The microwave oven was discovered in 1947 because Percy Spencer, an engineer working on radar for the military, noticed his chocolate bar melted near radar equipment. He started experimenting. Within two years, the first commercial microwave oven was on the market.

The Internet started as ARPANET — a 1960s US Defense Department project to keep communications alive after a nuclear strike.

GPS was a Cold War military navigation system. Today it powers Google Maps, Uber, DoorDash, Airbnb (to find your exact rental location), and essentially every logistics company on earth.

Drone warfare in Afghanistan and Iraq gave Amazon the operational playbook for Prime Air delivery drones.

**The pattern:** Military urgency funds technology at levels civilian markets never would. Once proven, that technology bleeds into civilian life — usually within 10-25 years, and increasingly faster as the dual-use pathway shortens.

Right now, the Ukraine conflict is funding AI targeting systems, counter-drone technology, satellite communications (Starlink became a military necessity before it was a consumer product), and autonomous systems. Those technologies are incubating the next decade of civilian infrastructure.

Defense tech isn't a defense play. It's an infrastructure play with a government subsidy.

### 4. Commodity Prices Spike on Fear and Collapse on Peace — Faster Than Anyone Expects

In July 1990, crude oil was $17 per barrel. By October 1990, after Iraq invaded Kuwait, it hit $46 — a 170% spike in three months.

The Gulf War coalition launched on January 17, 1991. Within days of early military success, the war premium began evaporating. By 1994, crude oil had dropped to its lowest price since 1973.

Let me repeat that: oil went from $46 to below $20 in roughly three years. Not because demand collapsed. Because the *fear premium* collapsed.

This pattern has repeated in every major conflict. Iraq War 2003: oil spiked pre-war, then moderated quickly. Ukraine 2022: oil hit $130+, then settled to $70-80 as markets adapted.

Today, Iranian conflict is adding a war premium to Brent crude. Gas prices are rising on fears of Iraq-era spikes. But US shale production is at record levels. Saudi Arabia has spare capacity. OPEC+ can open taps.

**Here's what nobody is writing about:** The Peace Trade.

Everyone writes about wartime opportunity. Nobody writes about how to position for peace arriving faster than expected. But the fastest, most violent market moves happen when peace arrives unexpectedly — oil collapses, defense stocks selloff, reconstruction plays activate.

The peace dividend is real, fast, and almost always mispriced because everyone assumes the conflict will last longer than it does.

### 5. Human Nature Doesn't Pause During Crisis — It Redirects

COVID killed more than 7 million people. The global economy contracted by trillions. And yet:

- Birth rates rose 6.2% among work-from-home-eligible women (people had children)
- Remote work went from 17% to 44% virtually overnight, then stabilized at 43% hybrid — permanently
- Mental health apps went from nice-to-have to essential infrastructure
- People moved to smaller cities and bought homes closer to nature
- Entrepreneurs started businesses at record rates in 2020 and 2021

Human beings don't stop wanting things during crisis. They want different things, in different ways, from different places. The economic activity doesn't disappear. It *redirects*.

Wars produce diaspora communities that eventually return and rebuild. They produce "never again" political will that reshapes policy for decades. They produce cultural shifts toward simplicity, meaning, and genuine human connection — all of which create new markets.

The desire to live fully does not wait for geopolitical stability.

## The COVID-to-War Translation Table

This is the frame that unlocked my thinking on this topic.

Look at what COVID looked like in real-time versus what it created in hindsight:

| What Looked Like Crisis (2020) | What It Became (2022-2026) |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Remote work chaos | Permanent hybrid shift — 43% of workers now hybrid |
| Travel industry destroyed | Airbnb IPO at $68B — 7x its crisis valuation |
| Mental health crisis | $5B+ mental health app market |
| Supply chain collapse | $300B+ supply chain tech investment wave |
| "Baby bust" fears | Baby boom among WFH-eligible women |
| Office real estate dying | Suburban and secondary city real estate boom |
| Telemedicine "experiment" | 40x telehealth market expansion |

Now look at what's happening in 2026 — and translate it the same way:

| What Looks Like Crisis Now | What History Suggests It Becomes |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| European energy dependency on Russia | €1T+ green energy independence investment |
| Defense tech urgency, runaway spending | Dual-use AI/autonomous systems — next civilian tech wave |
| Ukraine destroyed | $588B reconstruction pipeline, one of history's largest |
| Supply chains rerouting around conflict | Central Asia, Turkey, India logistics boom |
| Digital warfare and cyberattacks | Cybersecurity becomes critical infrastructure |
| Capital fleeing conflict zones | Dubai, Singapore, Switzerland strengthen as hubs |
| Oil price spikes from Middle East tension | Renewable economics permanently improved, accelerated adoption |

The silver linings are never visible in real-time. They require the exact same lens Silver Lake used when everyone was panicking about Airbnb: *where is this inevitably going?*

## Dubai: A Specific Case Study in Crisis Pricing

I want to be concrete, because abstract frameworks are easy to agree with and hard to act on.

Dubai's real estate market is the clearest current example of the fear-vs-fundamentals gap.

**The fundamentals:** Dubai recorded $250 billion in real estate transactions in 2025 — the highest in its history. Prices have risen 60-75% since 2021. Rental yields of 6-8% vastly outperform European markets. Zero income tax. Strong legal infrastructure. Golden visa program creating structural buyer demand.

**The fear:** Iranian drone and missile strikes in early 2026 targeted UAE infrastructure, including Dubai International Airport. The Dubai Property Index dropped 20%. Buyers are negotiating 2-7% discounts at closing. Transaction volume slowed.

Here's the first-principles question: **Why does Dubai exist as a financial center?**

Not because of geography. Not because of oil (Dubai's oil ran out decades ago — that's Abu Dhabi). Dubai exists because the UAE government made a strategic decision to build a neutral, tax-efficient, infrastructure-rich hub and has spent 40 years and hundreds of billions defending that decision.

The government of Dubai has more skin in the game protecting its "safe haven" status than any individual investor. That's not naïve faith — it's recognizing the incentive structure.

Every Middle East conflict of the last 30 years ended with capital flowing *to* Dubai, not away from it. Lebanon crisis → Dubai. Saudi Arabia political instability → Dubai. Egyptian economic crisis → Dubai.

**The probable scenario:** If the current conflict de-escalates by Q3 2026 — which historical patterns suggest is likely — Dubai pricing will recover and exceed previous highs by 2027. The 2-7% discounts available at closing today will be remembered the way people remember buying US equities in March 2020.

**The risk case:** If conflict escalates dramatically, with sustained UAE territorial targeting and genuine infrastructure collapse, a 15-25% correction is possible. That correction would also be the buying opportunity of the decade.

Either path leads to the same place: those with dry powder and patience will profit. Those who sold in fear will not.

## What This Means If You're Not Silver Lake

The most common objection to this framing is fair: "Silver Lake had $20 billion in reserves. I have a savings account."

True. And it changes the scale, not the principle.

The Silver Lake playbook for individuals:

**1. Maintain liquidity deliberately.** Crisis opportunities require cash, not leverage. The single biggest mistake most people make is being fully invested in normal times and having nothing left when assets are mispriced. A 10-20% cash buffer isn't underperformance — it's optionality.

**2. Make a list of assets you'd buy if they dropped 30%.** Not speculatively. Not greedily. Assets you understand fundamentally, believe in structurally, and would be comfortable holding for 5-10 years. Crisis is not the time to discover new asset classes — it's the time to buy the ones you've always wanted at the prices you've always wanted.

**3. Stop selling what you planned to hold.** The single most value-destructive behavior during crises is panic selling of fundamentally sound assets. Silver Lake didn't sell Airbnb when everyone else was dumping travel stocks. They bought.

**4. Watch where the diaspora goes.** This is the most underrated signal in geopolitics. When talent and capital flee a conflict zone, they carry information about which adjacent markets benefit. Ukrainian tech talent moving to Warsaw, Lisbon, and Berlin → those cities' tech ecosystems strengthened. Iranian capital moving to Dubai, London, Toronto → those real estate markets received structural support.

**5. Read the reconstruction, not just the destruction.** $588 billion in Ukraine reconstruction over a decade means concrete, steel, energy infrastructure, digital systems, housing. Those are very specific supply chains. The companies positioned in those supply chains during the conflict will not be the same ones everyone rushes to invest in after the peace deal is signed.

## The Harder Question

I started by saying I find it uncomfortable to write about opportunity during a time of genuine suffering.

I still do.

But I've come to believe the discomfort is important, not paralyzing. The capital that flows toward reconstruction *matters*. The companies that position to rebuild Ukraine, to stabilize Dubai, to fund dual-use tech that eventually becomes civilian infrastructure — they're not just extracting value from crisis. They're providing the capital that makes recovery possible.

Silver Lake's $1 billion in Airbnb wasn't charity. But it kept Airbnb alive as an employer of hundreds of thousands of hosts, gave it the runway to IPO, and ultimately returned value to everyone in the ecosystem — including Airbnb's workforce, which might have been entirely laid off if the company had collapsed.

Crisis capital has a moral dimension that pure profit-seeking obscures. The question isn't just "what can I make?" but "what am I funding?"

When the world is on fire, the people who run toward the smoke — with capital, with expertise, with patient conviction about the inevitable recovery — are the ones who shape what gets rebuilt.

The alternative — waiting on the sidelines for stability to return before investing — is also a choice. It just means someone else decides what gets built.

## What History Keeps Telling Us

I'll leave you with the simplest version of everything I've learned from studying crises:

**The fire is always real. The ash always grows things.**

Post-WWII ash grew the Marshall Plan, the Japanese economic miracle, the American suburban middle class, and 30 years of the highest broad-based prosperity in human history. The top 1%'s share of US wealth dropped from 15% to 8% — not because of socialism, but because the rebuilding created wealth broadly.

Post-COVID ash grew remote work, telehealth, Airbnb's resurgence, and a permanent reorientation of how people relate to work and place.

Post-conflict ash — from wherever the current fires eventually quiet — will grow something. The question history asks, over and over, is the same one:

*Were you positioned to plant in the ash, or were you still mourning the fire?*
